### SEGMENT 2: TEAM STRUCTURE & ROLES (1:30 - 4:00)
**Duration:** ~2.5 minutes

**VISUAL ELEMENTS:**
- Team settings interface
- Role hierarchy visualization
- Permission matrix
- Member list with roles
- Role assignment panel

**VOICEOVER:**
"First, let's talk about structure. A clear organizational structure prevents confusion and ensures people know their responsibilities.

Navigate to Settings > Team > Members. You'll see your entire team listed with their roles.

[Product Name] provides these default roles:

Admin: Full access. Can manage users, change settings, delete projects, and configure integrations. Typically, only a few people should be admins.

Manager: Can create and manage projects, manage team members within their scope, and access analytics. Good for team leads or department heads.

Contributor: Can create and edit items, participate in discussions, and view team data. This is your average team member role.

Viewer: Read-only access. Can see projects and discussions but can't make changes. Good for stakeholders or observers.

But here's the advanced move: create custom roles for your organization.

Maybe you have a 'Finance Approver' who can only approve budget items, or a 'Compliance Officer' who has read-only access but to sensitive data. Or a 'Contractor' with limited team visibility.

Click 'Create Custom Role' in the Team settings. Define exact permissions:
- Can create projects? Yes/No
- Can delete projects? Yes/No
- Can invite users? Yes/No
- Can access audit logs? Yes/No
- Can view team analytics? Yes/No
- Can manage integrations? Yes/No

This granularity ensures people have exactly the access they need—no more, no less.

As your team grows, think about org structure. Do you have departments? Teams within teams? [Product Name] supports nested teams.

Create a top-level 'Engineering' team and sub-teams for Frontend, Backend, and DevOps. Each sub-team can have different permissions and visibility.

A critical best practice: Principle of least privilege. Don't give everyone admin access. Don't make everyone a manager. Give people the minimum permissions they need to do their job.

Less access means:
- Fewer accidental deletions
- Less accidental configuration changes
- Clearer accountability
- More security

And importantly, communicate roles clearly. When someone joins, explain their role and what they can and can't do. Avoid confusion later."

### SEGMENT 3: COMMUNICATION & DISCUSSION (4:00 - 8:00)
**Duration:** ~4 minutes

**VISUAL ELEMENTS:**
- Comment threads within projects
- Discussion board interface
- Notification preferences panel
- Mention/tagging system
- Real-time typing indicators
- Message threading visualization

**VOICEOVER:**
"Communication is the lifeblood of teamwork. [Product Name] has powerful communication features built in.

First, comments on projects and items. Every item can have comments and discussion threads. Click an item to open it. Scroll to the Comments section.

Type your comment. Format it with **bold**, *italic*, and links. Mention a team member with @name. When you mention someone, they get notified immediately.

Comment threads are threaded, meaning replies to a specific comment are grouped together. This creates coherent conversations instead of a jumbled discussion.

Use comments for:
- Status updates and progress
- Questions and discussions
- Approval requests
- Design feedback
- Bug reports and discussions

Pro tip: Use reply threading instead of separate comments. If you're asking a follow-up question, click 'Reply' on the specific comment. Don't add a new top-level comment. Threading prevents 50 scattered comments and keeps conversations organized.

Next, mentions and notifications. The @ symbol is powerful. When you write:

'Hey @Sarah, can you review this proposal?'

Sarah gets notified. She knows you need something. She responds.

But be strategic with mentions. Mention only people who need to take action. Don't mention everyone on every comment.

Notification preferences are crucial. Each team member can customize their notifications to avoid being overwhelmed.

In Settings > Notifications, choose:
- 'All comments': You're notified of every comment in projects you're part of
- 'Mentions only': You're notified only when someone @mentions you
- 'Daily digest': Get one daily email summarizing all activity
- 'Off': No notifications (only for people who prefer checking in manually)

Pro tip: Different preferences for different notification types. Maybe you want:
- Project updates: Daily digest
- Mentions: Instant notification
- Task assignments: Instant notification
- Comments from others: Only when you mention

Next, discussion boards. Some conversations don't belong in a specific item. Use discussion boards for broader team discussions.

Go to a project and click the 'Discussions' tab. Start a new discussion. Title it clearly, like 'Q1 Goals Discussion' or 'Remote Work Policy Feedback.'

Team members join the discussion, post thoughts, upvote comments they agree with, and downvote ones they disagree with. This surfaces the best ideas naturally.

Discussions are great for:
- Brainstorming sessions
- Feedback gathering
- Policy discussions
- Decision-making
- Celebrating wins

Next, real-time collaboration. When multiple people work on the same item, [Product Name] shows you who's viewing it right now. You see typing indicators—'Sarah is typing...'—so you don't overwrite each other's work.

This prevents the classic problem: two people editing the same item simultaneously, with one person's changes getting overwritten.

Finally, communication best practices for your team:

1. Use threads. Don't create endless top-level comments. Reply to comments to keep discussions organized.

2. Be specific with mentions. Only mention people who need to take action.

3. Communicate decisions clearly. When a decision is made, post a summary to confirm everyone understands.

4. Use status updates. Regularly post updates on major projects. 'Awaiting stakeholder feedback,' 'On track for launch,' 'Blocked pending design approval.'

5. Archive completed discussions. Clean them up so important decisions remain visible and old clutter doesn't obscure them.

6. Use discussion boards for decisions. When making a team decision, use discussions to gather input rather than long email threads.

Communication done well keeps teams aligned. Communication done poorly leads to confusion and duplicated effort."

### SEGMENT 4: WORKFLOWS & ACCOUNTABILITY (8:00 - 12:00)
**Duration:** ~4 minutes

**VISUAL ELEMENTS:**
- Project status workflow visualization
- Assignment panel
- Due date highlighting
- Ownership clearly indicated
- Progress tracking dashboard
- Accountability metrics

**VOICEOVER:**
"Communication is important, but accountability is critical. Everyone needs to know:
- What they're responsible for
- When it's due
- How they're performing

Let's set this up.

First, clear assignment. Every item should have an owner or assignee. When you create a task, assign it to someone.

Click the Assignee field. Select a team member. Now that person knows they own this item. They get a notification. It appears in their 'My Work' or inbox.

Multiple assignments are possible. Maybe design and development both need to work on something. Assign it to both. Both get notified.

But avoid assigning to too many people. Three people max. With more, accountability dilutes.

Next, due dates. Set clear due dates for items. [Product Name] highlights:
- Green: Not due soon
- Yellow: Due soon (within 3 days)
- Red: Overdue

This creates natural urgency. Team members glance at the list and immediately see what's urgent.

Team leads can filter their project to show only overdue items and address them.

Next, status workflows. [Product Name] lets you define custom status stages for items. Default statuses might be:
- To Do
- In Progress
- In Review
- Done

But customize to your workflow. Maybe you're in support:
- New
- Assigned
- In Progress
- Waiting for Customer
- Resolved
- Closed

Or in design:
- Idea
- Wireframe
- Design
- Prototype
- Ready for Development
- Implemented

Move items through these statuses as they progress. This gives visibility into where everything stands.

Pro tip: Define what each status means. For 'In Review':
- Code review started
- Feedback provided to author
- No deployment until approved

Clear definitions prevent items from getting stuck in 'In Review' indefinitely with unclear status.

Next, accountability dashboards. As a manager, you want visibility without micromanaging.

Create a view that shows:
- All items assigned to each team member
- Due date for each
- Current status
- Days overdue if any

At a glance, you see:
- Sarah has 12 items, 2 are overdue
- Mike has 18 items, all on track
- Jessica has 8 items, 3 are due this week

This tells you where to focus. Maybe Sarah needs help. Maybe you need to prioritize Jessica's due items.

Next, performance metrics. [Product Name] can show you:
- Completion rate by team member
- Average time to complete items
- On-time delivery rate
- Workload distribution

Use this data to:
- Identify overburdened team members
- Recognize high performers
- Balance workload fairly
- Spot process bottlenecks

If items spend average 30 days in 'Design' stage but 2 days in other stages, there's a design bottleneck.

Next, one-on-one meetings. Use [Product Name] data in one-on-ones.

Review the employee's items together:
- What's going well?
- What's blocked?
- What do they need help with?
- How are they doing on deadlines?

This keeps conversations data-driven, not emotional.

Finally, team meetings. Weekly team meetings should reference [Product Name]:
- What did we complete this week?
- What's on track for next week?
- What's blocked or at risk?
- What decisions do we need to make?

Pull data from [Product Name] and discuss it together. This keeps meetings focused and action-oriented.

Accountability best practices:
1. Clear assignment—every item should have an owner
2. Clear due dates—no fuzzy 'someday'
3. Clear statuses—everyone understands workflow
4. Regular check-ins—status updates, not just reports
5. Data-driven conversations—use metrics to drive improvement
6. Help first—focus on helping blocked people, not punishing them

When accountability is clear and supportive, people thrive."

### SEGMENT 5: PERMISSIONS & VISIBILITY (12:00 - 14:30)
**Duration:** ~2.5 minutes

**VISUAL ELEMENTS:**
- Project visibility settings
- Shared project list
- Permission inheritance visualization
- Sensitive data access controls
- Audit log showing permission changes

**VOICEOVER:**
"Visibility and permissions go hand-in-hand. You want the right people seeing the right information.

First, project visibility. When you create a project, set its visibility:

Private: Only invited team members can see it. No one else has access. Good for sensitive projects.

Team: All team members can see it. But non-team members (contractors, clients) cannot. Good for internal projects.

Public: Anyone with the link can view it. Useful for public documentation or client-facing projects.

You can change visibility anytime. If you start with a private project and later want to share widely, no problem.

Next, shared access. Maybe you have a private project, but you want one specific person to see it. Invite them. They gain access.

When you invite someone to a project, choose their role:
- Owner: Full control over project
- Editor: Can make changes
- Commenter: Can only comment
- Viewer: Read-only

This granularity is powerful. Maybe a client should see your project but not edit it. Invite them as Viewer. They get status updates but can't make changes.

Remove access anytime. If someone was a contractor and their contract ended, click 'Remove' and they lose access immediately. This prevents former employees or contractors from seeing sensitive data.

Next, field-level permissions. Maybe you have a field with sensitive information—salary, SSN, password.

[Product Name] lets you restrict field visibility. Only people with appropriate roles see that field. Others see a [Restricted] placeholder.

Go to Project Settings > Fields. For sensitive fields, click the lock icon. Choose which roles can see it.

Now, when a non-authorized person views the item, they don't see 'Salary: $150,000.' They see 'Salary: [Restricted].'

This is critical for compliance. Maybe contractors should see project details but not budget information. Restrict budget fields. They work on the project without seeing financial data.

Next, audit logs. Every permission change is logged.

Go to Settings > Audit Log. Filter by 'Permission Changes.' You see:
- Who gained access to what
- Who lost access to what
- When these changes happened
- Who made the changes

This is essential for compliance. If you're audited, you can prove you revoked access timely when someone left.

Visibility and permission best practices:
1. Default to privacy—start private, open up only what's needed
2. Regular access reviews—quarterly, review who has access to what
3. Remove access timely—when someone's role changes, update access immediately
4. Principle of least privilege—give minimum access needed
5. Use roles strategically—don't make everyone admin
6. Document access decisions—explain in audit logs why someone has access

Balancing visibility and security keeps your team aligned while protecting sensitive data."

### SEGMENT 6: KNOWLEDGE SHARING & DOCUMENTATION (14:30 - 16:30)
**Duration:** ~2 minutes

**VISUAL ELEMENTS:**
- Knowledge base interface
- Documentation structure
- Search functionality
- Inline documentation in projects
- Template usage examples

**VOICEOVER:**
"A high-performing team shares knowledge. New team members should be able to learn from existing documentation, not by asking everyone questions.

[Product Name] has built-in knowledge sharing features.

First, wikis and documentation. Many projects have a 'Documentation' or 'Docs' tab. Use this to document:
- Project goals and objectives
- Key decisions and rationale
- Processes and workflows
- Technical architecture
- Contact information
- Common issues and solutions

Make it searchable. Organize it hierarchically. Link between documents.

When a new team member joins, point them to the documentation. They learn without interrupting people.

Next, templates. Repetitive projects follow the same pattern. Create templates to speed up new projects.

Design a project template for your type of work. Include:
- Standard items/tasks
- Default team members
- Default statuses
- Standard fields

When someone creates a new project, they choose your template. It auto-populates with standard items. No need to recreate from scratch each time.

This consistency is powerful. Every project has the same structure. New team members know what to expect.

Next, onboarding. Create an 'Onboarding' project for every new hire.

Include items like:
- Paperwork and admin
- Technology setup
- Security training
- System training ([Product Name], tools, etc.)
- Mentorship assignments
- First project assignment
- Check-ins (day 1, week 1, month 1)

Use this for every new hire. It ensures consistent onboarding. Nothing is forgotten.

Finally, document decisions. When your team makes a significant decision, document it.

Create a 'Decisions' discussion or wiki page. For each major decision, record:
- What decision was made
- Why (options considered, tradeoffs)
- Who decided
- When
- How it was communicated

This prevents 'why did we decide that?' arguments later. It's documented.

Knowledge sharing best practices:
1. Document as you go—don't wait until the end
2. Keep documentation updated—outdated docs are worse than none
3. Make it searchable—organize clearly
4. Link between docs—connect related information
5. Use templates—for consistency and speed
6. Record decisions—document important choices
7. Onboard consistently—every new hire gets the same experience"

- High priority interruptions
- Process bottlenecks

Address these systematically.

- What went well?
- What could we improve?
