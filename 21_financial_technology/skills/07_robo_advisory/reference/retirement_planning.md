# Retirement Planning Fundamentals

## Retirement Income Sources

### Primary Sources

**1. Social Security**
- Government benefit for eligible workers
- Monthly payment for life
- Starts age 62 to 70 (variable)
- Adjusted for inflation annually

**Amount Estimation**:
- Average benefit (2024): ~$1,850/month
- Full retirement age: 62-67 depending on birth year
- Early claiming (62): 70% of full benefit
- Delayed claiming (70): 124% of full benefit

**Strategy**:
```
High earner: Delay claiming to maximize survivor benefits
Mid-level earner: Claim at full retirement age (optimal in many cases)
Low earner: Claim early if health poor or liquidity needed
```

**2. Pensions**
- Employer-sponsored defined benefit plans
- Declining in private sector, common in public sector
- Typically monthly payment for life
- May include survivor benefits

**Types**:
- Single Life: Pays for life (stops at death)
- Joint & Survivor: Reduced payment, covers spouse
- Period Certain: Guarantees minimum period of payments

**3. Employment Income**
- Continued work into or during retirement
- Part-time or phased retirement
- Reduces portfolio withdrawal needs
- Provides psychological benefits

**4. Portfolio/Investment Income**
- Dividends and interest
- Capital gains from sales
- Withdrawal from accumulated assets
- Primary income for many retirees

### Secondary Sources

**1. Rental Income**
- From real estate ownership
- Ongoing monthly income
- Subject to maintenance and tax
- Illiquid asset

**2. Business Income**
- From self-employment or business sale
- Potentially significant but variable
- Tax complications
- Illiquidity considerations

**3. Annuities**
- Insurance products providing guaranteed income
- Useful for longevity risk
- Can be complex with various options
- Immediate vs. deferred annuities

## Retirement Savings Targets

### Replacement Ratio Approach

**Definition**: Percentage of pre-retirement income needed in retirement.

**Typical Ranges**:
- Conservative: 70-80% of pre-retirement income
- Moderate: 75-85% of pre-retirement income
- Aggressive: 85-95% of pre-retirement income

**Calculation**:
```
Current Income: $100,000
Replacement Ratio: 80%
Annual Retirement Need: $80,000
Accounting for inflation (2.5% × 30 years):
Future annual need: $80,000 × 2.1 = $168,000 (in future dollars)
```

### Multiple of Income Approach

**Target Savings by Age**:
- Age 30: 1x income
- Age 35: 2x income
- Age 40: 3x income
- Age 45: 4x income
- Age 50: 6x income
- Age 55: 7x income
- Age 60: 8x income
- Age 65: 10x income

**Example**:
```
Age 40, Income $100,000: Should have $300,000 saved
Age 55, Income $130,000: Should have $910,000 saved
Age 65, Income $150,000: Should have $1,500,000 saved
```

### Total Needs Analysis

**Components**:
1. **Years of Retirement**: How long will retirement last? (life expectancy + safety margin)
2. **Annual Expenses**: Total needed annually in retirement
3. **Income Sources**: Social Security, pensions, part-time work
4. **Inflation**: Adjust expenses for inflation
5. **Longevity Buffer**: Additional amount for extended life

**Calculation**:
```
Scenario: 65-year-old with 30-year life expectancy

Annual Expenses (today's dollars): $80,000
Annual Inflation: 2.5%

Inflation-Adjusted Expenses:
Year 1: $80,000
Year 2: $82,000
Year 3: $84,100
... (continuing for 30 years)

Total Needed (nominal dollars): $2,932,000

Less Income from:
Social Security: -$25,000/year × 30 = -$750,000
Pension: -$15,000/year × 30 = -$450,000

Portfolio Need: $2,932,000 - $750,000 - $450,000 = $1,732,000
```

## Retirement Account Types

### 401(k) Plans

**Characteristics**:
- Employer-sponsored retirement plans
- Pre-tax contributions (traditional) or after-tax (Roth)
- 2024 limit: $23,500 + $7,500 catch-up (age 50+)
- Employer matching (often 3-6% of salary)
- Vesting schedules vary

**Advantages**:
- Employer match (free money)
- Pre-tax contributions reduce current taxes
- Automatic payroll deduction
- Large contribution limits

**Disadvantages**:
- Employer control over fund choices
- Early withdrawal penalties (before 59.5)
- Required Minimum Distributions (RMDs) at 73
- Limited investment flexibility

### Traditional IRA

**Characteristics**:
- Individual retirement account
- Pre-tax contributions ($7,000 limit in 2024, $8,000 age 50+)
- Tax-deductible contributions (if eligible)
- Tax-deferred growth
- RMDs at age 73

**Advantages**:
- Full investment control
- No income source restrictions
- Flexible withdrawal options
- Tax-deductible contributions (if qualified)

**Disadvantages**:
- Lower contribution limits than 401(k)
- Income limits on deductibility (if covered by 401k)
- Tax-deferred growth creates future tax liability
- RMDs reduce flexibility in late retirement

### Roth IRA

**Characteristics**:
- After-tax contributions ($7,000 limit, $8,000 age 50+)
- Tax-free growth and withdrawals (5-year rule)
- No RMDs during account holder's lifetime
- Income limits on contributions ($161k-$176k single, 2024)

**Advantages**:
- Tax-free growth and withdrawals
- No RMDs during lifetime
- Can gift to heirs tax-free
- Greater flexibility in withdrawals
- Backdoor Roth strategy available

**Disadvantages**:
- After-tax contributions (no current deduction)
- Lower income limits for contributions
- Five-year rule limits early access
- Cannot use for early retirement income strategy

### SEP-IRA and Solo 401(k)

**For Self-Employed**:
- SEP-IRA: 25% of self-employment income or $69,000 (2024)
- Solo 401(k): $69,000 or higher with employee deferrals

**Advantages**:
- Higher contribution limits
- Simplified administration
- Flexibility in contributions

### Health Savings Account (HSA)

**Characteristics**:
- Triple tax advantage account
- Requires high-deductible health insurance plan
- 2024 limit: $4,150 individual, $8,300 family
- Can use for medical expenses anytime
- Can invest for long-term growth

**Strategic Use**:
- Don't use for current medical expenses
- Invest for growth
- Use in retirement for tax-free medical withdrawals
- Better than 401(k) for long-term retirement

## Retirement Income Projection

### Deterministic Model

**Simple Projection**:
```
Starting Portfolio: $1,000,000
Annual Withdrawal: $40,000
Annual Return: 6.5%
Inflation: 2.5%

Year 1: $1,000,000 × 1.065 - $40,000 = $1,025,000
Year 2: $1,025,000 × 1.065 - $41,000 = $1,051,625
Year 3: $1,051,625 × 1.065 - $42,025 = $1,080,041
... (continuing for 30 years)
```

### Stochastic Model

**Monte Carlo Approach**:
- Run 10,000+ simulations with varying returns
- Each year has random return from historical distribution
- Calculate success rate (% of scenarios maintaining portfolio)
- Show range of outcomes

**Output**:
- 10th percentile: Pessimistic scenario outcome
- Median: Middle outcome
- 90th percentile: Optimistic scenario outcome
- Success rate: % of scenarios achieving goals

### Tax-Adjusted Model

**Factors**:
- Ordinary income tax on distributions
- Capital gains tax on investment sales
- Qualified dividend treatment
- State income taxes
- RMD calculations and tax impact
- Tax bracket management

## Sequence of Returns Risk in Retirement

### Critical Period

**First 10 Years**: Most important for sustainable withdrawals
- Poor returns early reduce portfolio significantly
- Less time to recover before withdrawals
- Creates compounding negative effect

**Impact Example**:
```
Scenario A (Favorable):
Years 1-5: +10% annual return
Result: Portfolio grows despite withdrawals

Scenario B (Unfavorable):
Years 1-5: -5% annual return
Result: Portfolio shrinks, exhaustion risk higher

Same long-term average, different outcomes
```

### Mitigation Strategies

**1. Bond Tent**:
- Increase bonds entering retirement
- Hold 5-10 years of expenses in bonds
- Maintain equity exposure for growth
- Reduce need to sell stocks in downturns

**2. Flexible Withdrawals**:
- Reduce withdrawals in down years
- Increase withdrawals in up years
- Maintains portfolio longevity
- Requires discipline and flexibility

**3. Delayed Social Security**:
- Delay claiming if possible
- Creates guaranteed income floor
- Reduces portfolio withdrawal needs
- Provides inflation protection

**4. Phased Retirement**:
- Part-time work in early retirement
- Supplements portfolio withdrawals
- Reduces portfolio depletion rate
- Provides psychological benefits

## Longevity Planning

### Life Expectancy Considerations

**Planning Horizon**:
- Single person: 90-95 age target
- Couple: To age 95-100 for longer-living spouse
- Wealthy individuals: 100+

**Actuarial Estimates**:
- Age 65 male: 84 median, 94 (25% chance)
- Age 65 female: 87 median, 96 (25% chance)
- Age 65 couple: 93 (25% chance one lives to 100+)

### Longevity Insurance

**Deferred Income Annuities**:
- Purchase at age 65, payments start at 80+
- Guarantees income for life
- Protects against excessive longevity
- Irrevocable commitment

**Mechanics**:
```
Age 65: Buy annuity with $100,000
Age 80: Receive $600+/month for life
Cost: Foregone access to principal
Benefit: Guaranteed income for longevity risk
```

## Healthcare and Retirement Planning

### Medicare Costs

**Eligibility**:
- Age 65+, US citizen or permanent resident
- Medicare Parts A, B, C, D
- Coverage is not free
- Premiums and cost-sharing required

**Estimated Costs**:
- Annual premiums: $6,000-$12,000
- Deductibles and copays: $5,000-$10,000+
- Long-term care: $50,000-$100,000+
- Total healthcare needs in retirement: $200,000-$500,000+

### Planning Implications

- Include healthcare in retirement needs
- HSA as healthcare funding vehicle
- Long-term care insurance or self-insurance
- Consider health trajectory and family history

## Retirement Distribution Strategy

### Tax-Efficient Withdrawal Sequence

**Priority Order**:
1. **Non-Qualified Accounts**: Lower tax cost
2. **Tax-Deferred Accounts**: Manage RMD timing
3. **Tax-Free Accounts (Roth)**: Preserve for later

**Rationale**:
- Non-qualified accounts: Only pay tax on gains
- Tax-deferred accounts: Must eventually withdraw (RMD)
- Roth accounts: Tax-free for life and heirs

**Exception**: In low-income years, use traditional accounts to stay in lower bracket.

### Asset Location Strategy

**Bonds and Fixed Income**: Tax-deferred accounts (ordinary income taxation)
**Dividend Stocks**: Taxable accounts (qualified dividend rates)
**Growth Stocks**: Roth or taxable (long-term capital gains rates)
**International Stocks**: Taxable (foreign tax credit benefit)

## Common Retirement Planning Mistakes

1. **Underestimating Healthcare Costs**: Major overlooked expense
2. **Too Conservative Allocation**: Insufficient growth for long retirement
3. **Sequence Risk Ignorance**: Not understanding early-year return impact
4. **Social Security Timing Error**: Not optimizing claiming strategy
5. **Inflation Underestimation**: 2.5-3% long-term average
6. **Longevity Underestimation**: Planning to too-early age
7. **Early Retirement Risk**: Running out of money
8. **Tax Inefficiency**: Not managing withdrawal taxes
9. **Lifestyle Inflation**: Not reducing expenses after retirement
10. **Emotional Decisions**: Panic selling in downturns
