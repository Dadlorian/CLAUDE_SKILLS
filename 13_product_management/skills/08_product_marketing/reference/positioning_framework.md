# Positioning Framework: April Dunford's "Obviously Awesome"

## Introduction

April Dunford's positioning framework from "Obviously Awesome" provides a systematic, research-backed approach to developing positioning that actually works. Rather than relying on intuition or creative guessing, this framework walks you through a structured process to identify the most powerful positioning for your product.

## The Core Framework

### 1. Understand the Segments You Serve

**Objective**: Identify the types of customers that use your product and are most successful with it.

**Key Questions**:
- Who uses your product?
- Who gets the most value from it?
- Which customer segments grow faster?
- Which segments have better retention?
- Which segments expand (buy more) faster?

**How to Research**:
- Interview current customers about their use cases
- Analyze your customer database for patterns
- Look at Net Promoter Score (NPS) by segment
- Study case studies and customer wins
- Talk to sales and customer success about best customers

**Output**: A list of 2-4 customer segments that represent where you see the most value and growth potential.

**Example: Slack**
- Primary segment: Mid-market tech companies
- Secondary segment: Enterprise companies with distributed teams
- Not focused on: Single-team startups or non-tech companies

### 2. Identify the Right Frame of Reference

**Objective**: Determine what customers are comparing you to (implicit alternatives, competitors, or doing nothing).

**Key Insight**: Your positioning is relative to alternatives. Understanding what you're being compared to is critical.

**Frame of Reference Types**:

**Category Frame**: "We're the X for Y"
- Example: "Slack is the communication tool for distributed teams"
- Good when: You're creating a new category or the category is well understood
- Avoid when: You're being lumped in with products you're not similar to

**Functional Alternative Frame**: "We're better than X at Y"
- Example: "Figma is better than Sketch at collaboration"
- Good when: One clear alternative is what customers are comparing to
- Avoid when: You're more different than similar

**Job-Based Frame**: "We help you do X (that Y couldn't help with)"
- Example: "Notion lets you build a unified workspace (that disconnected tools couldn't)"
- Good when: You solve a different job than alternatives
- Avoid when: Customers don't see it as a different job

**Anti-Frame**: "We're NOT X"
- Example: "Stripe is NOT a hosted payment processor, it's the developer-friendly way to accept payments"
- Good when: You need to break a common misconception
- Avoid when: It seems defensive

**How to Find Your Frame**:
1. List what customers currently use (explicit alternatives)
2. Identify what customers would do without you (implicit alternatives)
3. Understand what decision framework customers use
4. Test different frames with customers to see which resonates most

### 3. Articulate the Unique Value

**Objective**: Identify what makes your product fundamentally different and more valuable.

**Types of Differences**:

**Feature Differences**
- Your product has something competitors don't
- Example: Figma's real-time multiplayer editing
- Risk: Easy to copy; may not be unique long-term

**Capability Differences**
- Your product can do things others can't
- Example: Stripe's ability to accept payments globally with one integration
- Better: Harder to copy, requires infrastructure

**Business Model Differences**
- How you deliver is different
- Example: Notion's single app for many use cases (vs. modular)
- Benefit: Creates switching costs and customer lock-in

**Approach/Philosophy Differences**
- Your fundamental approach is different
- Example: Basecamp's "less is more" philosophy vs. feature-heavy competitors
- Strong: Based on values and culture, hard to copy

**Target Customer Differences**
- You serve customers others don't serve well
- Example: HubSpot focuses on SMBs where Salesforce ignores them
- Sustainable: Built on focus and specialization

**How to Identify**:
1. List everything different about your product
2. Interview customers about why they chose you
3. Analyze win/loss records - what drives wins?
4. Compare side-by-side with top competitors
5. Get feedback from sales team on key differentiators

### 4. Connect Value to Specific Customer Needs

**Objective**: Show how your unique value solves real customer problems.

**Key Principle**: The value only matters if it solves something customers care about.

**Process**:
1. Interview customers about their biggest problems
2. Prioritize problems by importance and frequency
3. Map your unique capabilities to specific problems
4. Understand the job customer is trying to do
5. Show how your approach delivers better outcomes

**Example: Slack**

| Customer Need | Problem | Slack's Value | Outcome |
|---|---|---|---|
| Team coordination | Email is inefficient, messages get lost | Always-on, organized by channel communication | Faster decisions, less wasted time |
| Focus time | Constant interruptions from emails | Async-friendly, non-intrusive notifications | Deeper work, fewer distractions |
| Knowledge capture | Company knowledge is scattered and lost | Searchable history, organized conversations | Easier onboarding, less repetition |
| Cross-functional alignment | Teams don't know what others are doing | Transparent communication channels | Better alignment, fewer surprises |

### 5. Identify Your Proof

**Objective**: Find evidence that supports your positioning claims.

**Types of Proof**:

**Product Proof**
- Your product actually delivers on the promise
- Examples: Figma's architecture that enables real-time collaboration
- Most critical: Positioning must be credible based on product

**Market/Customer Proof**
- Market data showing the problem exists
- Customer data showing significant adoption
- Examples: Customer testimonials, case studies, metrics

**Analyst Proof**
- Third-party validation from trusted sources
- Examples: Gartner recognition, analyst reports
- Helpful: Provides credibility with enterprise buyers

**Competitive Proof**
- Showing where competitors are weak
- Examples: Comparison to alternatives in battle cards
- Use carefully: Should be factual, not misleading

**How to Build Proof**:
1. Product proof: Ensure product roadmap supports positioning
2. Customer proof: Collect customer testimonials and case studies
3. Market proof: Find or commission market research
4. Analyst proof: Pursue analyst relations and briefings
5. Historical proof: Accumulate wins with target customers

### 6. Define Your Positioning Statement

**Objective**: Create a clear, concise statement that brings everything together.

**Structure**:

"For [customer segment] who [statement of customer need], [product name] is the [frame of reference] that [unique value]. Unlike [alternative], we [proof point]."

**Example: Slack**

"For distributed teams who need to collaborate asynchronously, Slack is the always-on workspace for work that replaces email and scattered tools. Unlike email, Slack keeps conversations organized by topic, searchable, and accessible to the entire team."

**Example: Figma**

"For product and design teams who need to collaborate in real-time, Figma is the browser-based design tool that brings multiplayer editing to design work. Unlike desktop tools like Sketch, Figma enables entire teams to work together on the same file simultaneously."

**Good Positioning Statements Are**:
- Clear and specific
- Focused on customer needs, not product features
- Differentiated from alternatives
- Defensible and credible
- Easy for sales and marketing to use

**Bad Positioning Statements Are**:
- Too broad ("For everyone")
- Feature-focused ("We have X, Y, Z features")
- Vague ("Industry-leading", "Best-in-class")
- Unsubstantiated claims
- So complex that sales can't remember it

## How to Use This Framework

### Phase 1: Research (2-4 weeks)

1. **Segment Analysis**: Understand current customer base
2. **Customer Interviews**: Talk to 15-20 customers about how they use product
3. **Competitive Analysis**: Map alternatives and competitor positioning
4. **Win/Loss Analysis**: Study what drives wins and losses

### Phase 2: Development (1-2 weeks)

1. **Hypothesis**: Create 2-3 positioning hypotheses based on research
2. **Validation**: Test with 5-10 customers and sales team
3. **Refinement**: Refine based on feedback
4. **Documentation**: Create positioning statement and supporting narrative

### Phase 3: Rollout (2-4 weeks)

1. **Messaging**: Develop messaging hierarchy and key messages
2. **Internal Alignment**: Brief all customer-facing teams
3. **Sales Enablement**: Update sales materials and training
4. **Launch**: Roll out new positioning in marketing and sales materials

### Phase 4: Measurement (Ongoing)

1. **Awareness**: Track how well positioning is understood
2. **Preference**: Track competitive preference shift
3. **Conversion**: Track impact on win rates and conversion
4. **Feedback**: Gather ongoing customer feedback

## Common Positioning Mistakes

### 1. **Positioning for Everyone**
**Mistake**: Trying to appeal to all possible customer segments
**Result**: Weak, unfocused positioning that resonates with no one
**Solution**: Pick 2-3 primary segments and focus on them

**Example of Fix**:
- Bad: "Project management tool for teams of any size"
- Good: "Project management for distributed engineering teams"

### 2. **Feature-Based Positioning**
**Mistake**: Focusing on what product does rather than what customer achieves
**Result**: Indistinguishable from competitors with similar features
**Solution**: Focus on customer outcomes and benefits

**Example of Fix**:
- Bad: "Figma is a design tool with real-time multiplayer editing and version history"
- Good: "Figma brings multiplayer editing to design, enabling real-time collaboration"

### 3. **Positioning Without Proof**
**Mistake**: Making claims that product doesn't support
**Result**: Customers disappointed when product can't deliver
**Solution**: Ensure positioning aligns with product capabilities

**Example of Fix**:
- Bad: Position on feature that's in roadmap but not released
- Good: Position on features that exist and work well today

### 4. **Ignoring the Competition**
**Mistake**: Creating positioning without understanding competitive landscape
**Result**: Positioning that competitors can easily copy or that doesn't differentiate
**Solution**: Understand competitive positioning and find white space

**Example of Fix**:
- Bad: "Best-in-class project management tool" (everyone claims this)
- Good: "Project management designed for distributed teams" (specific differentiation)

### 5. **Inconsistent Positioning**
**Mistake**: Different teams (sales, marketing, product) using different positioning
**Result**: Confused customers, undermined credibility
**Solution**: Create shared positioning statement and messaging framework

### 6. **Static Positioning**
**Mistake**: Treating positioning as one-time exercise
**Result**: Positioning becomes stale as market evolves
**Solution**: Review positioning annually and when market conditions shift

## When to Reposition

**Signs You Need to Reposition**:
1. Customer feedback consistently shows misunderstanding
2. Win/loss analysis shows you're losing on your key differentiator
3. Market landscape has fundamentally changed
4. You're expanding to new customer segments with different needs
5. Competitive positioning has shifted significantly
6. Your positioning is limiting growth

**How to Reposition**:
1. Go through the framework again with fresh research
2. Don't abandon positioning without validation of new approach
3. Plan for 6-12 month transition period
4. Brief sales and customer success on change
5. Update all marketing and sales materials systematically

## Advanced Framework Applications

### Positioning for Different Buying Personas

Same product, different positioning for different buyers:

**Economic Buyer** (CFO)
- Focus on: ROI, cost savings, risk reduction, implementation timeline
- Positioning angle: Business value, financial impact

**End User** (Product Designer)
- Focus on: Ease of use, features, integration with their workflow
- Positioning angle: Productivity gain, better experience

**Champion** (Product Manager)
- Focus on: Strategic fit, competitive advantage, internal credibility
- Positioning angle: Enabling differentiation, reducing time-to-value

### Positioning for Different Markets

Same product may need different positioning in different markets:

**Vertical Markets**
- B2B SaaS for financial services: Focus on compliance and security
- B2B SaaS for healthcare: Focus on HIPAA compliance and healthcare workflows
- B2B SaaS for manufacturing: Focus on reliability and integration with legacy systems

**Geographic Markets**
- US market: Focus on innovation and features
- EU market: Focus on privacy, data protection, compliance
- Asia market: Focus on mobile-first, offline capabilities

### Multi-Product Positioning

When you have multiple products:

**Umbrella Positioning**: Parent brand positioning that unifies products
**Product Positioning**: Each product has specific positioning
**Ensure**: Products support umbrella positioning while being distinct

## Key Takeaways

1. **Positioning is Systematic**: Use April Dunford's framework for structured approach
2. **Customer Research is Critical**: Positioning must be based on real customer needs
3. **Differentiation Must Be Real**: Don't claim what your product doesn't deliver
4. **Segment Focus**: Better to be #1 in a segment than #2 overall
5. **Consistency Matters**: All teams must use same positioning
6. **Iterate and Evolve**: Revisit positioning as market changes
7. **Proof is Essential**: Back up claims with customer success and product capability
