# The Teresa Torres Continuous Discovery Interview Guide

Based on Teresa Torres' "Continuous Discovery Habits" - the definitive framework for regular customer conversations that drive product development.

---

## Core Philosophy

**Continuous discovery** is a discipline where product teams talk to customers every week, building a shared understanding that informs decisions daily. This guide teaches the interview structure that makes continuous discovery work at scale.

**Key premise**: You don't need long, perfect research projects. You need consistent, lightweight conversations with customers embedded in your team's rhythm.

---

## The Continuous Discovery Interview in 30-45 Minutes

### Pre-Interview Checklist

**Before scheduling** (with customer):
- [ ] Customer is relevant to your learning goal (not random)
- [ ] Scheduled for 30-45 minutes
- [ ] You're not trying to sell/close anything
- [ ] Explain the goal: "Learning how you approach [problem area]"
- [ ] No compensation needed (existing customers are happy to help)

**Before the call** (your prep):
- [ ] Define learning goal: "What do we need to learn this week?" (write it down)
- [ ] Develop 3-5 conversation prompts (not a rigid script)
- [ ] Know their role/background (from previous conversation or research)
- [ ] Set up quiet space (no distractions for you or them)
- [ ] Use notebook, not recording (feels less formal)
- [ ] Plan to take notes: key quotes, emotional reactions, surprises

**Your conversation setup**:
- Share screen only if showing specific product; otherwise video/audio only
- Note their emotional reactions (frustration, excitement, confusion)
- This is exploratory, not pitch; stay curious

## The Interview Structure (Teresa Torres Framework)

### Phase 1: Build Context (5-10 minutes)

Start with their world, not your solution.

**Opening statement** (1-2 min):
"Thanks for taking time to talk today. I'm learning how [product category users / people in your role / companies like yours] approach [problem area]. I'm not here to sell you anything—just want to understand your experience."

**Context questions** (ask 2-3, based on what you need to learn):

1. **Their recent experience**:
   - "Tell me about the last time you dealt with [problem area]"
   - "Walk me through what happened"
   - "When did this happen?"

2. **Their role/situation**:
   - "What's your role and what are you responsible for?"
   - "How long have you been in this position?"
   - "What's the typical workflow look like for you?"

3. **Their world/constraints**:
   - "How many people are affected by this?"
   - "How often does this situation come up?"
   - "What's the context when it happens?" (time pressure, tools available, etc.)

**Why start here**: You're learning about their actual situation, not jumping to your hypothesis.

**What to listen for**:
- Specific details (they're describing real experience, not theoretical)
- Emotional language (frustration, excitement = importance)
- How they describe the problem (their mental model)

---

### Phase 2: Explore Current Behavior (15-20 minutes)

Now explore how they currently handle it—their actual solution, not their desired solution.

**Current state questions**:

1. **How they currently solve it**:
   - "How are you dealing with this right now?"
   - "Walk me through your process step-by-step"
   - "What tools are you using?"

2. **What's broken**:
   - "What's hard about that process?"
   - "What's the biggest frustration?"
   - "What takes the most time?"
   - "What do you wish you could change?"

3. **Workarounds** (super valuable—reveals unmet needs):
   - "What's your workaround for [common problem]?"
   - "How do you get around [limitation]?"
   - "What do you do when [scenario] happens?"

4. **Frequency & scale**:
   - "How often does this happen?"
   - "How many people are involved?"
   - "How much time does this take you?"

**Why this phase matters**: Workarounds reveal real pain points. If they've built a workaround, the problem matters.

**What to listen for**:
- Time spent (if it takes hours, it's important)
- Manual steps (where is human effort required?)
- Multiple tools (where are gaps between systems?)
- Emotion/tone when describing workarounds (frustration = high pain)

**The critical phrase**: "Tell me more about that"
- Use it whenever they mention something interesting, vague, or emotional
- Let them expand; don't jump to solution

---

### Phase 3: Understand Their Desired Outcome (5-10 minutes)

What are they trying to accomplish? Not what features do they want, but what outcome matters.

**Desired outcome questions**:

1. **The actual goal**:
   - "What are you ultimately trying to accomplish with [task]?"
   - "If this were perfect, what would that look like?"
   - "What does success look like?"

2. **Why it matters**:
   - "Why is [outcome] important?"
   - "What happens if you don't achieve [outcome]?"
   - "Who else cares about [outcome]?"

3. **Constraints on solution**:
   - "What's non-negotiable? What constraints are we working with?"
   - "Are there compliance or regulatory requirements?"
   - "Who needs to be happy with a solution?"

**Why this phase**: Reveals whether they actually need what you're building, or if there's a deeper need beneath the surface.

**Example**:
- **Initial problem**: "Email templates take 2 hours to create"
- **Dig deeper**: "What are you trying to accomplish?" → "Get consistent messaging across our team's outreach"
- **Real need**: NOT faster template creation; it's consistent messaging. Maybe the solution isn't faster templates; maybe it's governance + template library + collaboration.

---

### Phase 4: Learning Review (2-3 minutes)

Before you wrap, check if you learned what you came to learn.

**Closing questions**:

1. **Validation**:
   - "Did I understand correctly that...?" (reflect back your learning)
   - "Is there anything I got wrong?"

2. **What you didn't cover**:
   - "Is there anything important we didn't talk about?"
   - "What would be helpful for me to understand better?"

3. **Relationship building**:
   - "Would it be helpful if I shared what we're learning?"
   - "Can I follow up if I have more questions?"
   - "Are there others on your team I should talk to?"

**Why this phase**: Ensures you learned what you needed; creates permission for follow-up.

## Documentation: Note-Taking During the Interview

You should be writing, but not transcribing. Focus on:

### What to Capture

**Direct quotes** (only for emotional/revealing quotes):
- "We've been dealing with this for 2 years and no one cares"
- "It's like death by a thousand cuts"
- These reveal emotion and mental model

**Key observations** (not full transcription):
- **Their behavior**: What they actually do (not what they say they do)
- **Patterns**: What's consistent across their situation
- **Workarounds**: What they've built to get around problems
- **Constraints**: What's non-negotiable
- **Emotion**: Frustration, excitement, resignation, resignation

**Your questions/surprises**:
- What contradicted your assumption?
- What surprised you?
- What do you need to learn more about?

### After Interview Notes Format

Within 2 hours of the call, write up:

```
INTERVIEW NOTES: [Name, Role, Company, Date]

LEARNING GOAL: [What we came to learn]

KEY OBSERVATIONS:
1. [Most important observation about their situation]
2. [Observation about their current process]
3. [Observation about what matters to them]
4. [Surprise or contradiction]

DIRECT QUOTES (if revealing):
- "[Emotional or insight-revealing quote]"

WORKAROUNDS DISCOVERED:
- [Any workarounds they've built]

UNMET NEEDS/PAIN POINTS:
- [What's clearly a problem for them]

QUESTIONS FOR FOLLOW-UP:
- [What we still need to understand]

SEGMENT/CONTEXT:
- Company size, industry, role
- How representative are they of your target?
```

**Keep it short**: 2-3 pages max. If you're writing more, you're over-analyzing during synthesis.

## The Key Interviewing Skills

### 1. Silence is Your Friend

After asking a question, **count to 10 in your head before speaking**.

Most people will fill silence with additional detail. That additional detail is often the real answer.

**Example**:
- You: "Tell me about the last time you struggled with this process."
- Them: "[30 seconds of explanation]"
- Silence...
- Them: "And honestly, what really frustrated me was that we had to do it three different ways..."

The second part (after your silence) is often more valuable than their initial response.

### 2. Reflect Back Your Understanding

Periodically summarize what you've heard:
- "So if I understand correctly, the real bottleneck is [X], and that's because [Y]?"
- "It sounds like [problem] is important because [outcome]—did I get that right?"

This does 3 things:
- Gives them a chance to correct misunderstandings
- Slows down to build deeper understanding
- Shows them you're listening carefully

### 3. Dig Into Contradictions

If they say one thing but described doing something different:
- Don't ignore it
- Gently probe: "I'm curious—you said X is important, but earlier you described doing Y instead. Help me understand how that works."
- Often reveals priorities or constraints they didn't realize they had

**Example**: "You said onboarding is easy, but you spent 2 weeks setting it up. What made that take so long?"

### 4. Separate Aspiration from Behavior

**What they say they should do** vs. **what they actually do** are different.

- If someone says "I review all reports daily" but also says "I usually skim the summary," believe the skim, not the daily review.
- If they say "cost doesn't matter" but chose the cheapest option, believe the behavior.
- If they say "ease of use is key" but still use a complicated tool, dig: why are they not switching if ease of use is so important?

**Probing technique**:
- "You said X is important, but I noticed you ended up doing Y instead. Help me understand what actually matters most when you're in the situation."

### 5. Learn to Ask "Why" Without Sounding Like a Toddler

Repeated "why" questions get annoying. Instead:
- "What was behind that decision?"
- "Help me understand..."
- "I'm curious about..."
- "Walk me through your thinking..."

These get at the same root cause without sounding interrogative.

### 6. Avoid Leading Questions

**Bad**: "Is the current process too slow?"
(They'll agree; nobody says "No, it's fine")

**Good**: "How long does that process take?"
(You get the actual data)

## Interview Scenarios

### Scenario 1: Discovering a New Problem Area

**Learning goal**: "What problems are small business owners facing in inventory management?"

**Interview approach**:
- Start broad: "Tell me about your inventory process"
- Explore their world: "How many SKUs, locations, team members?"
- Dig into problems: "What's frustrating about that?"
- Discover workarounds: "What's your workaround for [problem]?"
- Focus on outcomes: "If inventory management were perfect, what would that look like?"

**What you're learning**: Unprompted problems, real pain points, priorities

### Scenario 2: Validating a Solution Assumption

**Learning goal**: "Do users actually want automated invoicing?"

**Interview approach**:
- Ask about current behavior: "How are you currently sending invoices?"
- Probe what's hard: "What's the manual part? How often?"
- Ask about desired outcome: "What's the ideal process?"
- Don't mention your solution until they do: "Would automation help?"
- If they bring it up: "Tell me more about that"

**What to listen for**: Do they actually want automation, or is there a different underlying need?

### Scenario 3: Understanding Decision-Making

**Learning goal**: "How do small teams decide to adopt project management tools?"

**Interview approach**:
- Ask about their trigger: "What made you start looking at tools?"
- Explore their decision process: "How did you evaluate options?"
- Understand their constraints: "What were dealbreakers?"
- Learn about friction: "What almost prevented you from switching?"

**What you're learning**: Decision criteria, stakeholders involved, switching friction

### Scenario 4: Understanding a Competitor Loss

**Learning goal**: "We lost a deal to Competitor X. Why?"

**Interview approach**:
- Ask what happened: "Can you walk me through your decision process?"
- Understand their evaluation: "What were you looking for?"
- Learn what competitor did better: "What did they do that we didn't?"
- Probe switching friction: "What would make you reconsider?"

**What you're learning**: Competitive positioning, unmet needs, future opportunity

## Recruiting Customers for Interviews

### Who to Talk To

**Target recent/active users**:
- Contacted you in last 3 months
- Currently paying customers
- Power users who understand the problem well
- People who've abandoned/churned (why?)

**Avoid**:
- Friends and family (tell you what you want to hear)
- Professional research panel participants (professional responders behave differently)
- Only your happiest customers (not representative)
- Only your power users (not representative of typical user)

### Getting to "Yes"

**Email template**:
```
Subject: [Quick call about how you use X]

Hi [Name],

We're learning how [target segment] approaches [problem area], and I remembered you recently [action they took]. Would you have 30 minutes next week for a quick conversation?

I'm not pitching anything—just want to understand how you work and what matters most to you.

If you're open to it, let me know a few times that work for you.

Thanks,
[Your name]
```

**Keys to recruiting**:
- Specific reason why you're asking (shows you've done homework)
- Short ask (30 min feels doable)
- Clear it's not a pitch
- Easy logistics (offer flexible times)
- No incentive needed for existing customers

### Scheduling

- Aim for 1-2 interviews per week per team member
- Spread them out (don't do 5 back-to-back)
- Schedule same day/time recurring if possible
- Buffer 30 min before for prep, 30 min after for notes

## Team Practice: Interviewing Together

### How to Involve the Whole Team

**Why**: When designers, engineers, and PMs interview together, they all develop customer empathy.

**How to do it**:
1. **Someone leads the conversation** (usually PM)
2. **Others listen and take notes** (designers, engineers)
3. **After the call, debrief together** (5-10 min discussion)
4. **Everyone writes their own observations** (filters through their lens)

**In the debrief**:
- "What surprised you?"
- "What contradicted your assumption?"
- "What did you learn about their priorities?"

### Varying Roles

**Once a week rhythm**:
- **Monday-Tuesday**: PM interviews customers
- **Wednesday**: Designer and PM co-interview
- **Thursday**: Engineer and PM co-interview
- **Friday**: Team synthesis (1 hour)

This scales discovery: 3-4 interviews per week, whole team involved, shared learning

## Remote Interview Best Practices

### Video/Phone Setup

- **Quiet space**: No background noise on your end; ask them about theirs
- **Camera on**: Builds rapport; you want to see their face when they react
- **Close any distractions**: Email, Slack, other tabs
- **Minimal note-taking on screen**: They can see you typing; takes attention away from listening

### When to Use Phone vs. Video

- **Video** (preferred): Better rapport, see reactions, more conversational
- **Phone** (acceptable): If they prefer, easier to take notes, feels less formal
- **Async** (not ideal): If timing impossible, offer recorded message; gets 30% of value

### Dealing with Awkward Silences

Silence feels longer to you than to them. Count to 10. If still silent:
- "Are you still thinking?"
- "Take your time"
- Just wait another 3 seconds

Most people will jump in.

## Documenting Patterns: Weekly Synthesis

### Friday Synthesis Meeting (1 hour)

After interviews throughout the week, team synthesizes together.

**Structure** (1 hour):
1. **Set context** (2 min): "Here's what we were learning this week"
2. **Review notes** (20 min): Key observations from interviews
3. **Pattern identification** (20 min): "What are we seeing across conversations?"
4. **Implications** (10 min): "What does this mean for our roadmap?"
5. **Next week's learning goal** (5 min): "What do we need to learn next week?"

**Output**:
- Updated shared document of patterns discovered
- Any changes to thinking/priorities
- Learning goal for next week

### Tracking Patterns Over Time

Maintain a running document:
```
CONTINUOUS DISCOVERY: PATTERN LIBRARY

PROBLEM: [Major problem we've identified]
- First observed: [Date, who mentioned it]
- Observed again: [Date, who mentioned it]
- Observed again: [Date, who mentioned it]
- Status: Confirmed pattern (3+ mentions)

DECISION CRITERIA: [What matters when users evaluate solutions]
- Mentioned by: [Names and dates]
- How important: [Critical / Important / Nice to have]

WORKAROUND: [What users have built to get around a problem]
- Mentioned by: [Names and dates]
- Effort involved: [High / Medium / Low]

UNMET NEED: [Something users want but aren't getting]
- Mentioned by: [Names and dates]
- Segment: [Who experiences this need]
```

## Common Mistakes & How to Fix Them

### Mistake 1: Pitching Instead of Learning
**What happens**: You can't help yourself; you start talking about your solution

**Why it happens**: You're excited and want validation

**How to fix**:
- Print interview guide and stick to it
- Have team member keeping you accountable
- Remind yourself: listening builds more conviction than pitching
- If they ask about your solution, say: "I'll get to that, but first help me understand..."

### Mistake 2: Only Interviewing Power Users
**What happens**: You learn what enthusiasts want, but miss mainstream user needs

**Why it happens**: Power users are easier to recruit and more articulate

**How to fix**:
- Deliberately recruit average/light users
- Ask: "How often do you actually use [feature]?"
- If they say "monthly," include them

### Mistake 3: Asking What They Want Instead of How They Work
**What happens**: You get feature requests, not needs

**Why it happens**: It's the intuitive question

**How to fix**:
- Ask about behavior: "Tell me about the last time you [did X]"
- Ask about current solution: "How are you handling this now?"
- Only ask about desires after understanding their world

### Mistake 4: Not Taking Notes
**What happens**: You forget key details; you remember the memorable quote, not the common pattern

**Why it happens**: Feels like divided attention

**How to fix**:
- Write up observations immediately after call (within 2 hours)
- You don't need to transcribe; just capture key points
- Share notes with team within 24 hours

### Mistake 5: Interviewing Only Happy Customers
**What happens**: You miss why others churned or didn't adopt

**Why it happens**: It's easier to get them to agree to a call

**How to fix**:
- Actively recruit users who churned or evaluated and chose competitor
- Ask: "Why did you stop using us?"
- These conversations are gold for learning

## Quick Interview Checklist

Before each interview:
- [ ] Learning goal defined (what do I need to learn?)
- [ ] Customer is relevant (not random)
- [ ] I've prepped context about their situation
- [ ] Quiet space, minimal distractions
- [ ] Notebook ready
- [ ] Interview guide nearby (but not reading from it)
- [ ] I'm not planning to pitch

After each interview:
- [ ] Wrote up key observations within 2 hours
- [ ] Sent to team within 24 hours
- [ ] Marked anything surprising or contradictory
- [ ] Noted patterns with previous interviews
- [ ] Scheduled time to discuss with team

After weekly interviews:
- [ ] Held 1-hour synthesis with team
- [ ] Documented patterns in shared library
- [ ] Updated learning goals for next week
